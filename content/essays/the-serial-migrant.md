I have moved countries enough times to know that starting over is mostly paperwork; so * much * goddamn * paperwork. People imagine reinvention as something glamorous. A woman arrives in a new city with two suitcases, purchases a little fiddle-leaf fig, finds a "charming" café with all kinds of fake mylks, and becomes an entirely different person. In reality, she spends three weeks trying to open a bank account, discovers that her previous driving history does not exist in the eyes of the local insurance industry, and develops an unusually intimate relationship with a customer service representative (Oi João, tudo bem?! I miss you my one and true bb at EDP Comercial).

Also, nothing makes you question the continuity of the self quite like being told that you have no credit history.[^1]

I have had a life in Algeria, Australia, the UK, the Gulf, Portugal, and the United States, among other places. There have been different houses, jobs, friendships, languages, and versions of myself. Some of these lives overlap, while others have almost nothing to do with one another. I occasionally wonder whether they would get along if introduced.

## On becoming someone else

Migration is often discussed as a question of identity. Where are you from? Where do you belong? Which culture do you identify with? Who are you? These questions assume that identity is something relatively stable that migration disrupts. But I wonder whether migration also reveals just how unstable identity was to begin with.

Most people have different selves. There is the person they are with their parents, the person they are at work, the person they become around an old friend, and the person who comes out after two glasses of wine at a dinner party; where will you land between lawful good and chaotic evil? Migrants simply have more opportunities to observe the differences.[^2]

There is the version of me who speaks Arabic, the one who speaks French, and the one who conducts most of her professional life in English. These are not straightforward translations of one another. They have different rhythms, different cultural references, different tolerances for bullshit and nonsense. Certain feelings are easier to express in one language than another. Certain jokes only work in the language in which they were learned.

Certain versions of intimacy are almost impossible to reproduce once translated.

I can be the same person in three languages and still feel that something fundamental changes between them. Psychologists have studied how bilingual and multilingual people may express different aspects of personality across languages. Culture, context, and the relationships associated with a language can influence how we describe ourselves and behave.[^3] I find this both fascinating and stupidly inconvenient. Apparently, even the personality is subject to local conditions; like frizz.

## The reinvention industrial complex

There is a particular mythology surrounding starting over. Leave the relationship. Move to another country. Change careers. Cut your hair. Become the person you were always meant to be. I understand the appeal. There is something lovely and appealing about arriving somewhere nobody knows you.

Nobody remembers your embarrassing teenage decisions, knows the person you used to date, or has witnessed your failed attempts at becoming organised. You are free of an audience that knows your previous performances and did not rate. For a brief period, you can introduce yourself without burden. This can be genuinely liberating, especially when the person you have been is no longer the person you want to become.

But there is a small problem with reinvention: you keep coming with you.

It turns out that changing countries does not automatically change your attachment patterns, your anxieties, your relationship with money, or your tendency to become convinced that your life would be substantially better if you lived somewhere with more interesting architecture.[^4] You may exchange one set of circumstances for another, but your nervous system, habibi, there s/he is.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-serial-migrant/image16.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit C</figcaption></figure>

There is also something peculiar about the contemporary insistence that we should constantly be becoming better versions of ourselves. Reinvention is presented as both freedom and obligation, a personal project that is never quite complete. We are encouraged to transform our lives, optimise our routines, abandon outdated identities, and emerge as something more authentic, preferably with a new wardrobe and a compelling explanation for LinkedIn.[^5] At some point, one begins to wonder whether the problem is a failure to become oneself or an inability to leave oneself alone.

## The migrant as administrative fiction

One of the stranger aspects of migration is the way it converts a life into documentation. Your existence becomes a collection of certificates, entry stamps, employment contracts, medical records, bank statements, and letters confirming things you thought were self-evident. You have to prove where you lived, where you lived before that, and why you left. You must demonstrate your ties to one country while establishing your commitment to another. You must be mobile enough to migrate but stable enough not to worry anyone.

Immigration systems are particularly interested in continuity. They want addresses, dates, employers, family relationships, and evidence of legitimate intentions. But migration is frequently the product of discontinuity. Relationships end. Opportunities disappear. Families change. People fall in love, become parents, lose jobs, discover that they hate a city, or wake up one morning with an overwhelming desire to be somewhere else.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-serial-migrant/image10.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit D</figcaption></figure>

There is rarely a little box on any form for "I thought things would be different", although that has probably been one of the great engines of human migration: I fucking hate it here.[^6]

The bureaucratic self is a peculiar creature. It is coherent, chronologically consistent, and equipped with supporting evidence. It has never forgotten an address, misplaced a certificate, or experienced a period of profound uncertainty about its intentions. Whether this person bears any resemblance to the actual applicant seems largely beside the point.

## The belonging question

People often ask where I feel most at home. This is a great question, and I have become very good at giving unsatisfactory answers. Sometimes home is a particular city. Sometimes it is a language. Sometimes it is an afternoon with people who understand a reference without requiring a twenty-minute explanation. Occasionally it is a supermarket that stocks the right ingredients.

Belonging is an elusive thing, particularly when you have spent a substantial part of your adult life somewhere other than where you were born. The Algerian sociologist Abdelmalek Sayad wrote about migration through the idea of double absence: the condition of being absent from the society of origin while never becoming entirely present in the society of arrival.[^7] It is a powerful way of describing the migrant's predicament, although I sometimes wonder whether there is also a strange kind of double presence - is it being in both places or being neither here nor there.

There is the ability to carry several places within you simultaneously, to feel affection for somewhere you no longer want to live, to miss a country while remembering perfectly well why you left it, or to feel at home somewhere and still be regarded as foreign. You can return to your country of origin and discover that you have become a visitor there too.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-serial-migrant/image3.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit E</figcaption></figure>

There is no single emotional position that resolves these contradictions. You can love a place and leave it, remain shaped by somewhere you no longer live, and belong to a country without particularly wanting to return. Perhaps belonging is less about finding the one place where everything makes sense and more about accepting that different places have claims on different parts of you.

## The geography of regret

Every move creates an alternative life. There is the life you might have had if you had stayed in London, the career that might have developed in Dubai, the friendships that might have deepened in Australia, and the apartment you might still be living in if one particular relationship had worked out differently.

Migration multiplies the number of people you might have become. Psychologists Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius introduced the concept of possible selves to describe the imagined versions of ourselves that we might become, hope to become, or fear becoming.[^8] Most of us carry a few imagined alternatives. Serial migrants maintain an entire international portfolio.

The danger is that every place begins to represent something another place lacks. London has the cultural life, but not necessarily the sunshine. Australia has the beaches, but everyone is horribly far away. The Gulf has its extraordinary ambitions and incredible contradictions. Other cities offer different possibilities, different frustrations, and different fantasies of the person you might become there. Me every time I move:

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-serial-migrant/image7.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit F</figcaption></figure>

You can spend years comparing your actual life in one place with an imaginary life somewhere else. The imaginary life generally wins. Its rent is reasonable, its friendships are effortless, and nobody ever gets sick. It is an extremely unfair competition.[^9]

And perhaps there is something especially cruel about being able to imagine so many plausible futures. When you have lived in several places, the alternatives are not entirely imaginary. You know the streets, the languages, the rhythms of ordinary life. You can picture yourself buying groceries, meeting friends, collecting a child from school, growing older. These lives feel close enough to touch, which makes accepting the one you are actually living rather more complicated.

## The child who asks where home is

There is something about becoming a parent that changes the emotional geography of migration. When you are responsible only for yourself, a move can feel like an adventure, even when it is difficult. With a child, the calculation becomes a little bit more complicated. You begin thinking about continuity in ways that may not have occurred to you before: the languages they will speak, the friendships they will remember, the places that will become familiar, and the rituals that might survive a move.

You wonder how to give another person a sense of rootedness when you have spent so much of your own life in motion. And perhaps you begin to question whether freedom and stability are necessarily opposites.[^10]

A child can belong to several places. Plenty do. But belonging requires more than collecting countries. It requires relationships, repetition, recognition, and the ordinary experience of being known. Someone who remembers you. Somewhere you can return without having to introduce yourself again.

I find myself increasingly drawn to this idea. Not necessarily to a particular country, but to the possibility of accumulation. A life in which friendships are allowed to deepen, objects acquire histories, neighbours become familiar, and the same people appear in the same places year after year. A life in which things are allowed to stay.

## Is there a diagnosis for this?

Psychology has a vocabulary for many of the difficulties associated with migration: acculturative stress, identity negotiation, grief, social dislocation, adaptation. These are useful concepts, and there is a substantial body of research examining how migration shapes psychological well-being.[^11] But I am reluctant to turn every experience of rootlessness into a psychological problem.

Moving can produce suffering, absolutely. It can also produce pleasure, resourcefulness, curiosity, and an extraordinary capacity to recognise the arbitrariness of what other people regard as normal. Once you have lived within several social systems, it becomes harder to believe that any one of them represents the natural order of things. You discover that there are many perfectly respectable ways to raise children, organise households, conduct relationships, and live a life. You also discover that people are capable of being deeply irritating in every language and in every context. This is an important cross-cultural finding, but I have not found the relevant peer-reviewed literature yet.

There is also a question worth asking about the language of adjustment itself. If a person struggles to adapt to a world organised around immigration restrictions, precarious employment, social isolation, and the expectation of permanent flexibility, is the problem necessarily located in the person?[^12] Perhaps some difficulties are not signs of psychological dysfunction but perfectly intelligible responses to the conditions in which we live.

I am not trying to romanticise perpetual movement. Starting over is tiring. It can be lonely. And there comes a point when the excitement of reinvention begins to compete with the desire to own a sofa that will not have to be sold in six months. But I am not convinced that having lived several lives means I have failed to establish one.

Perhaps identity is not a single coherent story. Perhaps it is an archive, with some sections beautifully catalogued and others containing substantial gaps. A few documents are missing. Certain chapters are still too painful to examine. There are contradictory records, disputed accounts, and several versions of events; all are true.

The institution remains open despite longstanding administrative concerns.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-serial-migrant/image8.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit G</figcaption></figure>

I may still move again, or I may finally stay somewhere. Neither decision will resolve the question of who I am. But I have begun to think that the point of all these lives is not to keep becoming someone new. Perhaps it is to find a way for all the people I have already been to live together.

Preferably in a house with good light and lots of plants.

[^1]: **On the administrative extinction of the self.** A person may have decades of financial responsibility, paid rent on several continents, and successfully\* navigated multiple tax systems, only to show up in a new country and discover that, for credit purposes, she was apparently born last week. What the hell do you mean I can't get a credit card? How will I survive late state capitalism?!

[^2]: Came across Erving Goffman's The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1959). Goffman's dramaturgical approach is a useful way of thinking about the different selves we present in different social settings. Migration complicates the performance by changing not only the audience but also the stage, the script, and occasionally the language in which the entire production takes place. And scene.

[^3]: **On multilingual personalities.** See Ramírez-Esparza and colleagues, Do Bilinguals Have Two Personalities? A Special Case of Cultural Frame Switching (2006). Research suggests that bilingual individuals may report different personality characteristics depending on the language and cultural context in which they are operating. This does not mean multiple personalities in a clinical sense. It does suggest that the self is rather more context-dependent than we sometimes like to admit. Different vibe, different me.

[^4]: **On geographical solutions to psychological problems.** Further research is needed into the persistent belief that one's emotional difficulties might be resolved by relocating to a city with better architecture, a more agreeable climate, or a sufficiently sophisticated café culture; or a better vibe altogether. Preliminary autobiographical evidence is inconclusive.

[^5]: **On the reinvention industrial complex.** See the contemporary self-improvement economy, in which ordinary human development is repackaged as a continuous programme of personal optimisation. Also consult Carl Cederström and André Spicer's The Wellness Syndrome (2015). At what point did having a personality become an underperforming asset requiring constant management?

[^6]: **On the reasons people migrate.** Immigration applications tend to favour explanations involving professional opportunities, family reunification, education, and long-term planning. They are less well equipped to accommodate boredom, heartbreak, an intolerable winter, a poorly considered romance, or the suspicion that life might be more interesting elsewhere. I think these should also be recognised as legitimate forces.

[^7]: **On double absence.** Abdelmalek Sayad, La Double Absence: Des illusions de l'émigré aux souffrances de l'immigré (1999), in English The Suffering of the Immigrant (2004). Sayad, himself Algerian, insisted that immigration cannot be understood independently of emigration. The migrant is not simply someone arriving somewhere new, but also someone who has departed from another social world.

[^8]: **On the lives we did not live.** Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius, Possible Selves (1986). The concept describes the selves we imagine becoming, including hoped-for, expected, and feared possibilities. The interesting question for a serial migrant is whether geographical mobility expands this repertoire or merely makes the alternatives more difficult to abandon.

[^9]: **On the imaginary superiority of elsewhere.** The alternative life is unreliable. It remembers the summers and the sunshine but not the bureaucracy, the friendships but not the weekly cancelled plans, and the apartment's natural light but not its plumbing and/or mold. Send it home, it should not be allowed to testify.

[^10]: **On belonging, children, and continuity.** A useful question is whether rootedness must be geographical at all. For children raised across countries, continuity might also come from relationships, languages, familiar rituals, and predictable forms of care. The distinction matters because a childhood lived across borders need not be a childhood without roots. Equally, we should resist romanticising mobility when it is the child/ren who must repeatedly give up familiar people and places.

[^11]: **On acculturative stress.** John W. Berry's work on acculturation and acculturative stress provides a foundational framework for understanding the psychological demands associated with cultural transition. These demands vary considerably depending on social support, discrimination, economic conditions, and the wider receiving society. Migration is not a single psychological experience, and neither is adjustment.

[^12]: **On the politics of being well adjusted.** This is where the Institute's central suspicion comes back: whose conditions are we asking people to adjust to, and why is successful adaptation mistaken for psychological health? Ignacio Martín-Baró's liberation psychology offers an important counterpoint to approaches that detach individual suffering from political and social conditions. Not every failure to adjust is a failure of the individual; sometimes the environment deserves the diagnosis.
