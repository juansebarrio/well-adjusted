The sofa arrived three months after we did. It came in a container, wrapped in blankets my mother did not trust the shipping company to provide, and it has never once behaved like a guest.

This is the thing about furniture that has crossed a border. It does not adapt. It does not learn the language. It sits in a living room in a new country exactly as it sat in the old one, at the same angle to a television that is no longer there, facing a window that looked onto a different street.[^1]

My mother arranged the new flat the way you would restore a painting. Not decorated: restored. The sideboard went on the wall where a sideboard goes. The good glasses went inside it, behind the door that sticks, so that getting one out still requires the small private violence it has always required.

> We did not move house. We moved the house, and then stood inside it waiting to feel at home.

Psychologists have a word for objects that carry us across a gap. They call them transitional, and they mostly mean teddy bears.[^2] Nobody has written the paper on the transitional wardrobe: two metres of walnut that a family drags across a continent because it is easier to carry than the question of who they will be when they get there.

I used to find it embarrassing. Friends had flat-pack shelves and light. We had a dining table that could seat a wedding and a rule about coasters. It took me years to understand that the furniture was not refusing the new country. It was holding the old one in place, so the rest of us could go out and be foreigners all day and come back to somewhere that had not moved.

The sofa is in my flat now. It does not suit the room. It faces a window it has no opinion about. Guests sit on it and say it is very comfortable, in the tone people use for other people's religions.

I have not rearranged it. I am told this is a choice.

[^1]: The street in question had a bakery, a pharmacy, and a man who knew everybody's business. The new one has parking.
[^2]: Winnicott, mostly. He did not have to ship anything.
