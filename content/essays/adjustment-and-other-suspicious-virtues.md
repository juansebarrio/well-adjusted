There is a specific kind of person we are all supposed to become. They wake up early, meditate, drink something green, regulate their emotions, maintain appropriate boundaries, drink enough water, and have a working understanding of their attachment style. They are neither too attached nor too detached. They communicate their needs clearly, but not excessively. They are resilient, but not avoidant. Vulnerable, but not exhausting. Ambitious, but somehow also at peace.

They have done *The Work*™.

Nobody knows exactly what the work is, but apparently it is never finished.

I have been thinking about the phrase *well adjusted*. It sounds like something you might say about a child who has stopped asking difficult questions. It suggests that something once sat incorrectly and has now been brought into alignment with its surroundings.

But what if the surroundings are the problem?

How do you align in that kind of situation?

## A brief history of behaving yourself

Psychology has spent a considerable amount of time thinking about how humans adapt to their environments. Adjustment, broadly speaking, describes how we respond to the demands of life, negotiate difficulties, and manage relationships with ourselves and others.

A perfectly reasonable area of inquiry.

The trouble begins when we confuse successful adaptation with psychological health.

Historically, what counted as normal or healthy behaviour was not always distinguishable from what a particular society found convenient. Women were expected to be agreeable. Children were expected to be obedient. Workers were expected to be productive. Migrants were expected to assimilate. Anyone who objected too loudly risked being described as difficult, unstable, hysterical, or poorly adjusted.

The vocabulary has improved, thank God.

The expectations are less certain.

Today we have an entire industry devoted to helping people become better at tolerating their circumstances. We learn breathing exercises to survive unreasonable workplaces. We practise gratitude when we cannot afford rent. We are told to develop resilience in response to increasingly precarious lives.

There is nothing inherently wrong with breathing, gratitude, or resilience. I recommend breathing quite strongly.

But I wonder when we decided that the appropriate response to every difficult situation was to become psychologically better equipped to endure it.

At what point does coping become compliance?

## The emotionally regulated citizen

Consider the ideal contemporary adult.

She has identified her triggers, interrogated her childhood, learned to say no, and developed a morning routine involving some combination of movement, mindfulness, and an expensive powder or cream.

She no longer argues with people who misunderstand her. She protects her peace. She understands that other people's behaviour is a reflection of their own internal worlds.

She is, by all available metrics, doing extremely well.

She is also quite possibly furious.

But fury is not a particularly marketable wellness outcome.

We are encouraged to express anger constructively, disappointment proportionately, and grief within a timeframe that does not interfere too dramatically with our professional obligations.

Even our breakdowns must demonstrate emotional intelligence.

There is something exhausting about being asked to experience every feeling while simultaneously managing its presentation.

Sometimes anger is not a failure of regulation. Sometimes sadness is not a cognitive distortion. Sometimes anxiety is an accurate assessment of the situation.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/adjustment-and-other-suspicious-virtues/image21.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit C</figcaption></figure>

And sometimes you are being unreasonable. This is also possible. It is one of the less glamorous features of being alive.

The difficulty is knowing the difference.

## Freud would have been unbearable on Instagram

Freud's account of the human psyche was considerably messier than contemporary wellness culture tends to allow. People were contradictory, driven by desires they did not understand, and capable of sabotaging the very things they claimed to want.

He was wrong about plenty, but I appreciate his lack of optimism about our capacity for coherence. We are messy.

There is something reassuring about a psychological tradition that begins with the assumption that human beings are, at best, rather complicated.

Contemporary therapy language, by contrast, can sometimes make emotional life sound like an administrative problem.

Identify the pattern. Establish the boundary. Reframe the belief. Regulate the response.

Submit the appropriate documentation.

Receive confirmation that you are healing.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/adjustment-and-other-suspicious-virtues/image19.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit D</figcaption></figure>

Actual psychological work is rarely so tidy. People can understand their behaviour perfectly and continue doing it. They can know why they are attracted to unavailable people and still find unavailable people extraordinarily attractive and sexy. They can identify a harmful pattern, discuss it with great sophistication, and repeat it the following Tuesday. Tuesdays are my favorite days to act weird.

Self-awareness is useful. It is not magic.

An emotionally literate disaster remains a disaster.

Just one with better terminology.

## The problem with being functional

I have lived in enough places to notice that the definition of a functioning adult changes quickly once you cross a border.

In one society, independence is the highest achievement. In another, you are sus if you do not consult your entire family in the WhatsApp group before buying a sofa.

In some places, expressing your feelings is considered emotionally mature. Elsewhere, it is considered an unnecessary burden on everyone in the room; shut it down asap.

Some cultures celebrate ambition. Others regard excessive ambition as evidence that something has gone terribly wrong with your priorities.

And nearly everyone believes their own arrangement is the right one.

Which raises an inconvenient question: adjusted to what?

To capitalism? To the nuclear family? To the expectations of your parents? To the country in which you happen to hold a residence permit? To a romantic relationship? To the particular psychological fashions of the decade?

If adjustment means functioning well within a given environment, then the environment deserves rather more scrutiny than it usually receives.

A person who adapts beautifully to a profoundly unhealthy system may be considered successful.

A person who refuses may be considered difficult.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/adjustment-and-other-suspicious-virtues/image1.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit E</figcaption></figure>

History contains quite a few difficult people to whom we owe a lot.

This is not an argument for romanticising distress. I am not trying to say that suffering is not inherently profound. Some problems really do require treatment, and some behavioural patterns really do need to change. There is nothing emancipatory about being unable to sleep, maintain relationships, or get through the day. We got to live, at the end of it all.

But there must be some room between psychological suffering and compulsory contentment.

Somewhere we can admit that the world is strange, other people are exhausting, and we are not always delighted by the arrangements (toxic positivity be damned).

Perhaps the goal should not be to become perfectly adjusted.

Perhaps it should be to develop the capacity to distinguish between what we need to change about ourselves and what we have been persuaded to tolerate.

To understand our emotions without treating each one as a malfunction.

To recognise that a good life is not necessarily a frictionless one.

I have a massive problem with the idea that healing produces a permanently calm, endlessly compassionate person who has transcended the need to be annoyed.

Some people deserve our annoyance or anger or frustration and some situations deserve our refusal.

And some mornings you are not dysregulated. You simply have forty-seven emails, an unpaid invoice, an annoying child, and a garbage truck who sounds like they've parked right in front of your home specifically.

I don't know what a fully adjusted person looks like, certainly not me.

I am more interested in people who remain curious about their contradictions. People who can laugh at themselves without dismissing their suffering. People who understand that being alive is a complicated and occasionally humiliating business.

People who are trying, without necessarily attempting to become a finished product.

Which brings us to this Institute; my little made up world.

It has no accreditation, no clinical authority, and no intention of improving the general population.

It is interested in psychology, migration, identity, behaviour, and the delulu lengths human beings will go to avoid admitting what they want.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/adjustment-and-other-suspicious-virtues/image12.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit G</figcaption></figure>

Its central research question is simple:

What if you're not poorly adjusted? What if it's all weird?

Further investigation is required.
