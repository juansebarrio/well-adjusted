Your mother calls you three times. You don't answer. She calls again, and you finally pick up.

"Why weren't you answering?"

"I was busy."

"Busy doing what?"

"Working."

"Have you eaten?"

"Yes."

"What did you eat?"

You describe your lunch. She lets you know that was actually not a real lunch. You remind her that you are an adult. She asks whether you are angry with her. You say no. She says you sound angry. You become angry.[^1]

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-family-group-chat/image23.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit B</figcaption></figure>

Somewhere on the internet, a person is preparing to describe this interaction as a textbook example of narcissistic abuse, and I would like everyone to calm TF down. Not because the interaction is necessarily healthy, but because the speed with which we have begun diagnosing our relatives is actually quite alarming.

## The family has entered the group chat

We live in a golden age of psychological vocabulary. People who once described their ex-partner as selfish now describe them as narcissistic. An unpleasant disagreement becomes gaslighting.[^2] An awkward conversation becomes emotional invalidation. Someone who fails to reply to a message may be avoidantly attached, emotionally unavailable, or engaged in a subtle campaign of manipulation. Psychology has furnished us with an impressive range of explanations for why other people are so difficult to live with; why are people people-ing damn it??[^3]

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-family-group-chat/image22.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit C</figcaption></figure>

Hold on now, sometimes these descriptions are accurate. Gaslighting is real. Emotional abuse is real. Personality disorders are real. So are destructive family dynamics that people have spent years struggling to name. The language of psychology has given many people a framework for recognising suffering that might previously have been dismissed or normalised, and that is no small achievement. But there is a difference between using psychological concepts to understand relationships and turning every irritating and annoying human interaction into a diagnostic event.

A person can be selfish without having narcissistic personality disorder, just as a parent can be intrusive without being abusive. A disagreement can be unpleasant and/or annoying without being traumatic, and someone can remember an event differently without deliberately attempting to destabilise your grasp on reality. These distinctions matter, particularly when psychological language travels across cultures and into families where the boundary between individual autonomy and collective obligation has never been especially tidy.

## The boundary has not been approved by the family

Contemporary therapeutic culture places considerable emphasis on boundaries, and usually for very good reasons. People should be able to refuse unwanted demands, protect their privacy, and distinguish their own responsibilities from those of others. Estrangement is the default. Protect your peace, bb.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-family-group-chat/image6.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit D</figcaption></figure>

Yet the language of boundaries is not culturally neutral.[^4] It fits particularly well with social arrangements in which the individual is understood as the primary unit of moral and psychological life: you have needs, you have preferences, you have a right to make decisions independently, and other people must respect the limits you establish. Fine. Now explain this to my mother, who considers my nutritional intake, employment prospects, and choice of winter clothing to be matters of legitimate family concern. I have been having the same argument with my mother about needing to wear socks inside the house for the past 40 years. I'm still not doing it. Socks inside is yuck.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-family-group-chat/image25.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit E</figcaption></figure>

I come from a North African family and have spent much of my adult life moving between cultures with quite different ideas about what families owe one another. In some of these worlds, the suggestion that your parents should simply mind their own business would be received as a fairly radical proposition. What business? You are their business.[^5] This is not to suggest that North African families, or families elsewhere in the Mediterranean, Khaleej, or beyond, all operate according to a single cultural script. They don't. Households differ enormously across generations, classes, circumstances, and individual personalities. But there are social worlds in which autonomy is not necessarily understood as the highest expression of adulthood.

In many families, love is expressed through involvement. Care means asking questions, responsibility extends across generations, and decisions are understood to have consequences beyond the individual making them. A parent who leaves you entirely alone might not be considered respectful; they might be considered indifferent.[^6] And yet the same forms of involvement that communicate care can also become instruments of control. The difficulty is that these behaviours can look remarkably similar from the outside, and, to be honest, occasionally from the inside too.

Take the apparently innocent question, "Where are you going?" It might mean I love you and want you to be safe. It might mean I believe I am entitled to monitor your movements. It might mean I am anxious and do not know how to manage it. It might, rather inconveniently, mean all three.[^7] Human motivation is rarely considerate enough to present itself in clearly labelled categories, which makes the work of distinguishing care from control considerably more difficult than an Instagram carousel would have us believe.

## Freud meets the family WhatsApp group

Moving between different social worlds means encountering different understandings of what it is to become an adult. In one, independence is treated as an essential marker of maturity; in another, adulthood may involve becoming more deeply responsible for other people rather than less. In one, leaving home represents healthy separation; in another, maintaining close proximity to family may be evidence of maturity, loyalty, and care. Neither system is automatically superior, and both are capable of producing extraordinary kindness alongside spectacular psychological difficulties.

An intensely individualistic culture may provide freedom while leaving people isolated. A family-oriented culture may provide belonging while making independence feel like betrayal. Those navigating between the two inherit a rather exhausting assignment: be independent, but remain devoted; establish boundaries, but don't be selfish; honour your parents, but don't repeat their mistakes; heal your childhood, but don't embarrass the family in that process.[^8] The problem is not simply that these demands contradict one another. It is that each comes with its own account of what a good person should be, and people are often expected to satisfy all of them simultaneously. Good luck with that.

## Not every mother is a villain

The internet has become particularly fond of the emotionally immature mother.[^9] She is controlling, critical, demanding, manipulative, unable to respect boundaries, and deeply invested in maintaining access to her adult children's lives. There are mothers who behave this way, and some cause enormous harm. Psychological abuse does not become acceptable because it occurs within a particular cultural framework. Still, I am uncomfortable with how readily complicated family relationships are flattened into stories of villains and survivors, especially when mothers so often find themselves cast as the villain.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-family-group-chat/image9.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit G</figcaption></figure>

We ask women to devote so much of their lives to caring for other people, then express surprise when some of them struggle to relinquish the role. We build family structures around maternal sacrifice, reward women for making themselves indispensable, and accuse them of being controlling when they continue behaving as though they are indispensable. This is not an excuse for harmful behaviour. It is a reminder that people do not develop in a vacuum, and that what appears to be an individual psychological failing may also have a social history.[^10]

A woman who has spent decades being told that her value lies in caring for her children may experience their independence as more than a practical change. It may unsettle her entire understanding of who she is. That does not mean her children should surrender their autonomy to protect her feelings, or that motherhood grants anyone an exemption from accountability. It does suggest that understanding the dynamic requires more than diagnosing her as problematic. We might also ask what kind of social arrangement produced a woman whose identity became so thoroughly entangled with the lives of other people, and why we are quicker to identify her behaviour as a personal defect than to examine the expectations that helped produce it.

## The therapy-speak problem

There is something peculiarly satisfying about finding a psychological term that explains another person's behaviour. It transforms confusion into certainty and gives the speaker a position from which to interpret everybody else. Your father is avoidant, your mother is narcissistic, your sister is emotionally dysregulated, and your ex is a gaslighter. You, naturally, are healing. The terminology creates a convenient asymmetry: other people have pathologies, while we have insights.[^11]

I think this is one of the more seductive features of therapy-speak. It lets us occupy the position of the person who understands, and understanding can become a form of power. If I can explain your behaviour through a psychological framework, I may no longer feel obliged to take your explanation of yourself seriously. You are not disagreeing with me; you are projecting. You are not upset; you are triggered. You do not have a different perspective; you are engaging in a defence mechanism. Perhaps. Or maybe I am wrong, a possibility that deserves considerably more attention than it usually receives.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-family-group-chat/image13.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit I</figcaption></figure>

Psychological knowledge should make us more curious about the complexity of other people, not less. It should also make us suspicious of our own certainty. People are notoriously unreliable narrators of their internal lives, including people who have read a great deal about attachment theory. There is something rather ironic about using the language of emotional awareness to avoid the discomfort of recognising that someone else may have a legitimate perspective. A psychological interpretation is still an interpretation, not a court ruling; which is also questionable truth be told.[^12]

## But culture is not an alibi

There is an equally dangerous argument on the other side: that controlling or intrusive behaviour should be accepted because it is cultural. "That's just how our families are." "She only does it because she loves you." "He grew up in a different generation." These statements may explain some messed up/weird/questionable behaviours, but they do not necessarily justify them. Respect for cultural difference does not mean people have to stay or accept harmful relationships, nor should it ignore the fact that members of the same family may experience its expectations very differently.

A family arrangement can give one person comfort and security but make another member feel trapped. Gender matters, as do generation, economic dependence, and the distribution of power. A daughter may have restrictions that her brother never faces. Someone whose housing or financial security depends on relatives has much less freedom to negotiate boundaries than someone who can simply move out. These differences are not marginal details that can easily be understood under the heading of tradition; they actually determine whose needs are accommodated and who is expected to adjust.[^13]

There is a difference between a mother asking what you ate and a parent systematically undermining your independence. Psychology can help us recognise that difference; culture can help us understand why the behaviour takes a particular form. Neither should be used to dismiss the other. I am not interested in replacing one simplistic explanation with another, because your mother is a narcissist is not automatically more sophisticated than this is simply how mothers are in our culture. Both can become ways of avoiding the harder work of understanding what is actually happening; which is the actual useful part.

## An alternative to diagnosing your mother

I wonder if we should begin with a less satisfying but more useful set of questions. What is actually happening in this relationship, and what does each person believe they are doing? What does the behaviour mean in the context of this particular family? Who has power, who is being hurt, and what needs to change? Can that change happen without requiring us to decide that one person is fundamentally defective? These questions are less immediately gratifying than assigning a label, but they have the advantage of directing our attention towards the relationship rather than a verdict about someone's character.

Sometimes the answer will be no. Some relationships are abusive, some people repeatedly violate boundaries, and some family dynamics cannot be repaired through better communication, empathy, or an appreciation of cultural context. Distance may be necessary, and nobody should be required to just deal with harm in the name of understanding another person's upbringing. But sometimes the answer is less dramatic. People may love one another and be terrible at expressing it; what feels suffocating to one person may feel like care to another. A parent may need to learn that an adult child is allowed to make decisions they dislike, while the adult child may need to recognise that concern is not always an attempt at domination. And sometimes everybody needs to stop discussing the damn issue at every dinner.

## The mother remains undiagnosed

I should clarify that mothers are not a psychological category. Neither are fathers, although I suspect an investigation into their emotional repertoires could keep this Institute occupied for several decades. Nor are North African, Mediterranean, or so-called collectivist families interchangeable. No particular family structure is inherently loving, dysfunctional, oppressive, or healthy. The point is not to defend the family against psychology, but to defend psychological complexity against the temptation to explain everything too quickly and too easily.

<figure class="exhibit"><img src="/essays/the-family-group-chat/image18.png" alt="" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Exhibit J</figcaption></figure>

A useful psychological framework should help us see both the genuine harms families can cause and the ways love, obligation, dependence, and care can be shaped by culture. It should make room for contradiction. Your mother can love you profoundly and still behave in ways that hurt you. You can understand why she behaves that way and still ask her to stop. You can establish a boundary without declaring her a narcissist, maintain a relationship without agreeing to every demand, or decide that the relationship is neither safe nor sustainable. None of these decisions requires a clinical label applied from the other side of the dinner table.

As for the original telephone exchange, I cannot give a definitive assessment. Further information is required. The mother has declined to participate in the study, on the grounds that the researcher has not eaten what they consider an actual meal and should call back later after they've eaten.[^14]
